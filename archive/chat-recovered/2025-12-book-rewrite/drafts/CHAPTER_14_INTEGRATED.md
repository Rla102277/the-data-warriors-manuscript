# CHAPTER 14
## See the Invisible: The Forces That Shape Decisions

*"The Void is that which has no beginning and no end. Attaining this principle means not attaining the principle. The Way of strategy is the Way of nature. When you appreciate the power of nature, knowing the rhythm of any situation, you will be able to hit the enemy naturally and strike naturally."*  
— Miyamoto Musashi, *The Book of Five Rings*

Musashi's Void Book is the hardest to grasp because it's about what you can't see directly—the invisible principles that govern all combat. Not techniques. Not tactics. The underlying structure that determines outcomes before the fight begins.

In data work, the Void is the invisible layer beneath everything: the incentive structures, the unspoken cultural rules, the real power dynamics, the career trajectories that determine whose recommendations land and whose disappear.

You can't see the Void directly. But once you learn to sense it, you understand why technically perfect analysis fails while mediocre recommendations succeed. Why some analysts advance rapidly while others stagnate despite equal skill. Why organizations make irrational decisions that are perfectly rational once you see the invisible forces at play.

Most analysts never perceive the Void. They optimize for what's visible—better models, cleaner code, more rigorous analysis—while remaining blind to the invisible forces that actually determine outcomes.

This is why brilliant analysts fail and mediocre analysts succeed. The brilliant ones see the data. The successful ones see the Void.

---

## What the Void Contains

The Void isn't mystical. It's the layer of reality that's real but not visible in org charts, project plans, or data models.

**The Void contains:**

**Incentive structures:** What people are actually rewarded for, versus what they claim to value. The VP says they want data-driven decisions, but they're promoted based on quarterly revenue. Which do you think drives their behavior?

**Power dynamics:** Who actually makes decisions versus who appears to make them. The CEO might approve the budget, but the CFO controls what gets proposed. Where's the real power?

**Cultural patterns:** The unwritten rules about how work gets done. In some organizations, consensus is mandatory. In others, speed beats perfection. In others still, process adherence trumps outcomes. You don't see these rules written down. You learn them by violating them.

**Career trajectories:** The invisible paths to advancement. In some organizations, technical depth gets rewarded. In others, business acumen matters. In others, political skill dominates. The org chart doesn't tell you this. You have to see it.

**Risk tolerance:** What failures are forgivable versus career-ending. Missing a deadline might be fine. Being wrong publicly might be fatal. Or vice versa. The employee handbook won't tell you which is which.

All of this is invisible. None of it appears in your analysis. All of it determines whether your work creates value or dies in email threads.

---

## Seeing Incentives

People do what they're incentivized to do. Not what they're told to do. Not what they say they'll do. What they're rewarded for doing.

**The executive who says "we need to think long-term" but gets bonused on quarterly earnings will optimize for quarters.**

**The manager who says "quality matters" but gets promoted based on shipping speed will cut corners.**

**The VP who says "innovation is critical" but gets punished for failed experiments will avoid risk.**

None of these people are lying. They genuinely believe what they're saying. But their behavior follows incentives, not intentions.

**To see incentives, ask:**

- What gets rewarded in practice, not in theory?
- What gets punished?
- Who got promoted recently, and what were they doing before promotion?
- Who got fired or sidelined, and what were they doing?
- When resources are scarce, what gets funded and what gets cut?

The answers reveal the real incentive structure. Which might be completely different from the stated values.

**Once you see incentives, you can predict behavior.**

If analysts get promoted for building impressive models regardless of business impact, expect complex models solving irrelevant problems.

If executives get rewarded for hitting quarterly targets regardless of long-term consequences, expect short-term optimization.

If managers get punished for failed projects more than rewarded for successful ones, expect risk aversion and political cover.

This isn't cynicism. This is seeing the Void.

---

## Seeing Power

Org charts show formal authority. The Void shows real power.

Formal authority is who approves decisions. Real power is who frames the options, who controls information, who has the CEO's ear, who influences before decisions reach the approval stage.

**The analyst who presents to the VP has formal authority.** The CFO who briefs the VP before your presentation has real power.

**The committee that approves budgets has formal authority.** The person who writes the budget proposal has real power.

**The executive who signs off on strategy has formal authority.** The consultant they trust has real power.

**To see power, ask:**

- Whose recommendations get implemented with minimal pushback?
- Who gets consulted before decisions are made?
- Who can kill projects without formal authority to do so?
- Whose calls get returned immediately?
- Who do executives ask "what do you think?" rather than "what are the numbers?"

The answers reveal where power actually resides.

**Once you see power, you can route your work appropriately.**

Don't waste time convincing people with formal authority but no real power. Find the people with real power and influence them.

This isn't manipulation. This is understanding how decisions actually get made.

---

## Seeing Culture

Every organization has unwritten rules about how work gets done. Violate them, and your work fails regardless of quality.

**In consensus cultures,** unilateral decisions get rejected even when correct. You need buy-in from stakeholders before proposing solutions.

**In hierarchical cultures,** bottom-up recommendations get ignored. You need executive sponsorship before building analysis.

**In process cultures,** following procedure matters more than outcomes. Perfect analysis delivered through wrong channels dies.

**In speed cultures,** slow rigor loses to fast directional guidance. Ship quickly even if imperfect.

**In political cultures,** technical merit is secondary to stakeholder management. Your analysis is a weapon in someone else's fight.

None of this is documented. You learn it by watching what succeeds and what fails.

**To see culture, ask:**

- Do people get punished more for process violations or outcome failures?
- Are decisions made by individuals or groups?
- Do junior people's ideas get heard or do they need senior sponsorship?
- Is speed valued over thoroughness, or vice versa?
- When someone succeeds, what did they actually do that led to success?

The answers reveal the cultural operating system.

**Once you see culture, you can work within it instead of against it.**

If you're in a consensus culture, involve stakeholders early. If you're in a hierarchical culture, get executive sponsorship first. If you're in a process culture, follow the process even when it's inefficient.

This isn't compromise. This is strategic adaptation.

## Seeing Career Paths

Some organizations reward technical depth. Others reward business acumen. Others reward political skill. Others reward nothing and select randomly.

**To see career paths, look at who got promoted:**

- Were they the most technically skilled?
- Were they the most politically connected?
- Were they the best at stakeholder management?
- Were they the ones who delivered business impact?
- Were they the ones who avoided failure, or the ones who achieved success?

The pattern reveals what actually gets rewarded.

**Common patterns:**

**Technical meritocracy:** The best modelers, cleanest coders, most rigorous analysts advance. Rare in business organizations. Common in tech companies and research institutions.

**Business impact meritocracy:** People who demonstrably drove revenue or reduced costs advance. Common in high-performing commercial organizations.

**Political meritocracy:** People who build alliances, avoid making enemies, and manage up effectively advance. Common in large, mature organizations.

**Tenure meritocracy:** People who stay longest advance regardless of performance. Common in government and some large corporations.

**Random selection:** No discernible pattern. Common in dysfunctional organizations.

**Once you see the pattern, you can optimize for it—or leave for an organization with a pattern you prefer.**

## Why Most Analysts Can't See the Void

The Void is invisible to people who only look at visible things.

**Analysts are trained to analyze data.** The Void isn't in the data.

**Analysts are trained to be objective.** The Void is subjective, political, and cultural.

**Analysts are trained to focus on technical excellence.** The Void determines whether technical excellence matters.

So most analysts remain blind. They build perfect analysis that fails for invisible reasons. They get frustrated when politics beats data. They stagnate in their careers while less technically skilled colleagues advance.

Not because they're bad analysts. Because they can't see the Void.

---

## How to Develop Void Perception

Seeing the Void is a skill. It can be learned.

**Practice 1: Watch outcomes, not intentions.**

Don't listen to what people say. Watch what they do. Watch what gets rewarded. Watch what gets punished. Watch who advances and why.

The gap between stated values and observed outcomes reveals the Void.

**Practice 2: Follow the money and promotions.**

When budgets get allocated, where does the money go? When people get promoted, what were they doing beforehand?

Resources and recognition flow toward what organizations actually value.

**Practice 3: Map informal networks.**

Who talks to whom? Who trusts whom? Who influences whom? The informal network often matters more than the org chart.

**Practice 4: Study failures.**

When projects fail, what was the real reason? Not the official explanation. The actual dynamic that killed it.

Failed projects reveal the Void more clearly than successful ones.

**Practice 5: Ask "why?" five times.**

Why did that decision get made?  
Why did they prioritize that over this?  
Why did that person get promoted?  
Why did that recommendation get rejected?  
Why does this pattern keep repeating?

By the fifth why, you're usually in the Void.

---

## Using Void Perception

Once you can see the Void, you have options invisible to others.

**You can route around resistance by understanding power dynamics.**

**You can frame recommendations to align with incentive structures.**

**You can work within cultural patterns instead of fighting them.**

**You can optimize your career for the advancement pattern that exists, not the one that should exist.**

**You can choose organizations where the Void aligns with your values.**

This isn't cynical. This is strategic.

You can't change the Void directly. But once you see it, you can work with it instead of against it.

And that's the difference between analysts who remain frustrated and analysts who create sustained impact.

## SWORD DRILLS

*To develop Void perception:*

1. **Map your organization's incentive structure.** List the last five people who got promoted. What were they doing that led to promotion? List the last three projects that got funded. What pattern explains why they got resources? Compare stated values to observed rewards.

2. **Identify real power.** List five recent significant decisions in your organization. For each, identify: Who had formal authority to decide? Who actually influenced the decision? Who got consulted first? Who could have killed it? Where does real power reside versus formal authority?

3. **Decode your culture.** Review the last five projects in your organization (successful and failed). For each, identify: Did it succeed/fail based on technical merit or cultural fit? What unwritten rules did it follow or violate? What does the pattern reveal about how work actually gets done?

4. **Trace career paths.** Study the last three analysts or data professionals who advanced in your organization. What did they do that led to advancement? Technical excellence? Business impact? Political skill? What pattern emerges? Does it match what you're optimizing for?

5. **Practice the five whys.** Pick one recent decision that surprised you (a rejected recommendation, a funded project, a promotion, a strategic shift). Ask "why?" five times. Document your answers. What invisible force in the Void explains the outcome?
