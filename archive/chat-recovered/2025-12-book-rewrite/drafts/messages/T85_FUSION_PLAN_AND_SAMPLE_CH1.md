## The Five Questions

Before writing a single line of code, before requesting data access, before scheduling stakeholder meetings—ask five questions:

**1. What decision is this analysis supposed to inform?**

Not "what insights might be interesting." What specific decision will someone make differently based on your work?

If you can't answer this, you're building a dashboard for a dashboard's sake. Stop. Find the decision first.

**2. Who actually makes that decision?**

Titles lie. Org charts lie. The person with "decision authority" often isn't the person who decides.

Find the human who will actually act on your analysis. Everyone else is context.

**3. What does success look like to them—not to you?**

You might think success means a 95% accurate model. They might think success means a recommendation they can defend to their boss in 30 seconds.

Your definition of success is irrelevant. Theirs is everything.

**4. What constraints exist that you don't see yet?**

Budget limits. Political dynamics. Technical debt. Regulatory requirements. Data availability.

Every project has invisible constraints. Surface them early or they'll surface themselves late.

**5. What happens if you're wrong?**

Some decisions are reversible. Some aren't. Some wrong answers cost thousands. Some cost millions.

Understanding the stakes calibrates how much rigor you need. Perfect accuracy on a reversible decision is waste. Directional guidance on an irreversible decision is malpractice.

---

## When the Ground Keeps Shifting

The ground isn't static. Business priorities change. Stakeholders rotate. Competitive threats emerge. What was true when you started might be obsolete when you finish.

**Every two weeks, re-ask the five questions.**

If the answers have changed, your project needs to change. Continuing down the original path after the ground has shifted is how projects die—beautifully executed answers to questions nobody's asking anymore.

**Three signals the ground has shifted:**

**1. Your stakeholder stops responding to emails.**  
They've moved on. The decision window closed or the priority changed. Find out why before continuing.

**2. Someone asks "is this still relevant?"**  
That's not a rhetorical question. That's a warning shot. Pause and validate the problem still exists.

**3. Your deadline extends without explanation.**  
When urgency evaporates, so has the business need. Don't confuse "nice to have" with "must have."

---

## When to Pivot vs. When to Finish

Sometimes the ground shifts and you should pivot. Sometimes you should finish anyway.

**Pivot when:**
- The original decision is no longer being made
- A bigger problem has emerged that invalidates your work
- Stakeholder consensus has evaporated

**Finish when:**
- The problem still exists but priorities have shuffled
- You're 80% done and the remaining 20% is polish
- The work builds foundational understanding for future projects

The analysts who finish every project regardless of relevance waste time. The analysts who pivot at every stakeholder whim never ship. Learn the difference.
