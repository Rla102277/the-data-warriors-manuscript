# PREFACE
## The Warrior and the Weight

There's a moment in every data professional's career when you realize the numbers can't save you.

Mine came on a zip line platform in the Dominican Republic.

---

## The Collapse

The year 2023 was the year my personal system experienced catastrophic failure. I was approaching 270 pounds. My marriage ended in divorce. And every Tuesday night, I'd sit in my home office preparing supply chain optimization decks for Fortune 500 clients while my own life collapsed around me.

I could map data lineage across five enterprise layers. I could build predictive models that forecasted consumer behavior with 94% accuracy. I could present to C-suite executives without breaking a sweat.

But I couldn't see the destructive pattern in my own life. The irony wasn't lost on me: I was running diagnostics on everyone else's broken systems while my own was failing catastrophically.

The data was clear. Weight: 270 pounds—legacy software nearing obsolescence. Sleep: four hours a night. Exercise: zero. Emotional state: running on empty, throwing good energy after bad decisions, refusing to acknowledge the corrupted baseline.

"Garbage in, garbage out" applies to more than databases.

---

## The Moment of Decision

Before I found the path to the high country, there was the low point. The number on the scale—270 pounds—was one thing, but the truly damning data was functional. It wasn't a single moment of quiet reflection; it was a series of humiliating, inescapable failures witnessed by the people I loved.

The first was on vacation in the Dominican Republic. I was with my daughter, ready to try a zip line course. The whole experience was a brutal audit of my physical decline. I struggled, huffing and sweating, just to get up the first three flights of stairs to the platform. Then came the panic: looking down the cable, I fixated on the horrifying thought of being the fat guy who gets stuck halfway, dangling over the jungle canopy, needing a rescue crew. The physical reality of my size and breathlessness was threatening to ruin a shared memory. I made it across, but the terror wasn't the height—it was the failure of the machine I inhabited.

The second moment, shortly after, cemented the decision. We were on a banana boat ride, one of those ridiculous, fun, but chaotic water tubes. Naturally, the operator decided to dump us into the water. As I tried to pull myself back up onto the inflatable tube, my arms and core simply failed. I was flailing, unable to generate the necessary leverage to hoist my weight. The boat left, and I was forced to watch it speed away, realizing I couldn't get back on. I had to shamefully swim back to shore alone while the rest of the group circled back, laughing, but seeing my failure.

My professional identity was built on sustained excellence, yet my body was failing at the most basic demands of human existence—playing with my daughter and pulling myself out of the water. The physical reality was a perfect, damning reflection of my compromised emotional and strategic state. That was the corrupted baseline I could no longer ignore.

I had to rebuild the machine from the ground up.

## The Samurai in My Living Room

It started with an impulse purchase on Amazon: a 1954 Japanese film about Miyamoto Musashi, the legendary swordsman who wrote *The Book of Five Rings* in 1645. I have no idea why I bought it. Maybe I was looking for something—anything—that felt like clarity in the middle of chaos.

One Tuesday night, exhausted from another twelve-hour workday, I pressed play.

The film froze on a single frame: Musashi's face, focused and calm, as he prepared to duel. I looked from the screen to my cluttered whiteboard, where I'd been struggling all day with a retailer's data integration project. The numbers weren't lining up. I was three weeks into a four-week project with nothing coherent to show.

And then Musashi spoke through the subtitles: "The data is the noise; the structure is the truth."

Actually, that's not what he said. But that's what I heard.

I sat up.

Holy shit. This samurai from 1645 understands my data problems better than my MBA professors ever did.

I'd been fighting with the shadow—the messy, contradictory data—instead of attacking the source: a broken data structure that would never yield clean insights no matter how much I cleaned it. The problem wasn't my analysis. The problem was the foundation.

I spent the next four hours rebuilding the entire data model from scratch.

By 2 AM, the structure was clean. The insights fell into place effortlessly. The presentation practically wrote itself.

That's when I started reading *The Book of Five Rings*. Not as ancient philosophy, but as an operations manual for modern data work.

## The First Decade: Learning by Breaking Things

My first decade in analytics—late 90s through the mid-2000s—was spent at Fortune 500 consumer goods companies and retail consulting firms. I believed one thing with religious fervor: more data equals better decisions.

I was wrong.

I built 100-page decks when the CEO needed one path forward. I created dashboards with forty metrics when leadership needed visibility into three. I optimized forecasting models to 99.8% accuracy when the VP just needed 80% certainty on the one thing they could actually change.

The turning point came during a project for a global tech manufacturer. I'd spent three months building a supply chain optimization model. It was beautiful—game theory, Monte Carlo simulations, scenario planning across eighteen variables.

The VP looked at my presentation for maybe thirty seconds.

"I don't need 99.8% accuracy," he said. "I need 80% certainty on the one thing I can change right now."

He was right. And I'd wasted three months building the wrong thing.

That's when I stopped being a Chief Data Modeler and became a Chief Simplicity Officer. My job wasn't to show everything I knew. My job was to answer one question: "What single action should we take right now?"

The work got simpler. And infinitely harder.

Because the final presentation isn't a report. It's an act of persuasion backed by data. And persuasion requires understanding people, not just numbers.

## The Physical and The Professional

I lost 85 pounds through trekking and half-marathons. From 270 pounds to 185.

Guadalupe Peak in West Texas. High-altitude hikes in the Colorado Rockies. Half-marathons in cities I'd never visited before.

Here's what I learned: The trek is the big strategic shift. You plan for months. You train. You manage resources. You face terrain you've never seen before. You deal with altitude, weather, and the mental challenge of mile after mile when your legs are screaming to stop.

The half-marathon is daily discipline. It's showing up every morning even when you don't want to. It's hitting your splits even when it's boring. It's the unglamorous work that builds the capacity for the dramatic moments.

Data work is both.

You need the trek—the big strategic initiative that requires months of planning and sustained effort under pressure. And you need the half-marathon—the daily discipline of clean code, reliable processes, and showing up even when the work isn't glamorous.

Most analysts chase the treks and ignore the half-marathons. They want the big project, the impressive model, the executive presentation.

But endurance isn't intensity. Endurance is career longevity. And career longevity is built in the boring, consistent, daily work that nobody celebrates.

## Why This Book

There's a gap in data analytics that costs companies millions and destroys careers.

It's not technical. The math is solved. The tools work. The frameworks exist.

The gap is strategic discipline.

I've watched $300,000-a-year analysts create algorithms that die in SharePoint folders. I've seen executives ignore brilliant insights because they were presented at the wrong time to the wrong audience. I've watched companies invest millions in data platforms that generate zero business value because nobody asked the right question before building the solution.

This book exists to close that gap.

It's written for three audiences:

**The powerless analyst** who knows the right answer but can't get anyone to listen. You're technically brilliant. You've done the work. But your insights die in email threads while executives make worse decisions based on gut feeling. You need to understand how decisions actually get made—and how to position your work inside that reality.

**The frustrated executive** who hired smart people and bought expensive tools but still can't get a straight answer to a simple business question. You're drowning in dashboards that show everything and clarify nothing. You need analysts who think strategically, not just technically.

**The overwhelmed entrepreneur or mid-level manager** who knows data could transform their business but has no idea where to start. You don't need a PhD in statistics. You need a framework for asking the right questions and knowing when you have enough information to act.

If you're any of these people, this book is for you.

---

## The Two Demands

I'm going to make two demands of you as a reader.

**First: Demand simplicity.**

Not simple answers. Simple questions.

When someone presents you with analysis, ask one question: "What is the single action I must take right now?"

If they can't answer that in one sentence, the analysis isn't done. Send them back.

**Second: Embrace discipline.**

Strategy without execution is daydreaming. The perfect insight is useless until it is flawlessly executed. Execution is boring. Execution is repetitive. Execution is showing up Tuesday after Tuesday, building the thing, testing the thing, fixing the thing, presenting the thing.

That's the work.

That's always been the work.

## What You'll Find in This Book

Miyamoto Musashi's *The Book of Five Rings* is organized into five books, each representing a different aspect of strategy and combat. I've structured this book the same way, but translated into the language of data analytics:

**The Ground Book** teaches you to see the problem clearly before you build the solution. Most projects fail here—not in execution, but in diagnosis.

**The Water Book** teaches you to adapt. Data work is fluid. Requirements change. Stakeholders pivot. Systems break. Rigidity kills projects. Flow keeps them alive.

**The Fire Book** teaches you to win the room. Your analysis doesn't matter if nobody acts on it. This section is about persuasion, timing, and strategic communication.

**The Wind Book** teaches you what not to do. I've consolidated the common failure patterns—the bad habits, broken processes, and organizational dysfunction that destroy data initiatives.

**The Void Book** teaches you to see the invisible structure beneath everything—the political dynamics, the unspoken incentives, the cultural patterns that determine whether your work succeeds or dies in silence.

Each section includes stories from my career: the failures that taught me, the successes that surprised me, and the moments when I had to choose between being right and being effective.

Because that's the real work. Not building the perfect model. Building the model that changes the decision.
