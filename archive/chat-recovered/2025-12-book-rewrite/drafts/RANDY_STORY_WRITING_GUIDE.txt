Randy, these stories are THE MOST IMPORTANT PART of making this book work.

Everything else—the frameworks, the techniques, the Musashi references—exists in other books. Your stories don't exist anywhere else. They're what will make readers remember this book and recommend it to colleagues.

GENERAL GUIDELINES FOR ALL STORIES

**Length**: 300-500 words each (except Preface which is 2,500)

**Voice**: Conversational. Write like you're telling this to a colleague over coffee. Use "I." Use contractions. Be honest about mistakes and failures.

**Structure** for each story:
1. Set the scene (1-2 sentences: when/where/what)
2. The challenge/conflict (2-3 sentences: what went wrong or what was at stake)
3. What you did (the middle: your actions, decisions, thought process)
4. The outcome (what happened)
5. The lesson (what you learned - make this explicit)

**What to include**:
- Specific details (numbers, names of tools, real reactions)
- Your internal thought process ("I thought... but actually...")
- The human element (how people reacted, what they said)
- What you'd do differently now

**What to avoid**:
- Company names (use "a Fortune 500 consumer goods company" or "a major retailer")
- Real people's names (use roles: "the VP," "my manager," "the CEO")
- Anything that could violate an NDA
- Excessive technical jargon
- Being too modest (own your wins) or too arrogant (own your losses)

**Tone**: Self-aware. You're an experienced practitioner reflecting on hard-won lessons, not a guru dispensing wisdom.

STORY #3: STATED VS. REAL PROBLEM (Chapter 2)
TARGET: 400 words

Describe a time when the stated problem was completely different from the real problem.

**Example prompts** (pick one that resonates):
- They said "analyze why sales are declining" but the real problem was "prove that it's not my department's fault"
- They said "we need a dashboard" but the real problem was "I don't know how to talk to my team about performance"
- They said "help us understand customer churn" but the real problem was "our product sucks and we don't want to admit it"

**Structure**:
- What they asked for (the stated problem)
- What you initially did (analyzed the stated problem)
- How you discovered the real problem (what tipped you off?)
- How you reframed the project
- The outcome (did solving the real problem work? or did it create new problems?)

STORY #4: WRONG TOOL FOR THE JOB (Chapter 3)  
TARGET: 300 words

**Option A**: A time you over-engineered something
- Built a complex solution when simple would have worked
- What made you realize you'd over-complicated it?
- What was the cost (time, credibility, confusion)?

**Option B**: A time you under-engineered something
- Used too simple an approach when you needed more sophistication

- When did you realize you needed a more robust solution?

Pick the one that makes you cringe more when you remember it. That's the one to write.

WRITING TIPS

**Block time**: Don't try to write all of these in one sitting. Write one story per day over 11 days.

**Write bad first drafts**: Get the story down, even if it's clunky. We'll polish in Week 5.

**Read out loud**: If a sentence sounds weird when you say it, rewrite it.

**Be specific**: "I spent three months" is better than "I spent a long time." "The VP said 'this is bullshit'" is better than "The VP disagreed."

**Show, don't tell**: Not "I was frustrated" but "I went home and threw my laptop across the room."

**End with the lesson**: Make it explicit. Don't make the reader guess what they're supposed to learn.
